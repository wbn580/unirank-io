University rankings are the most influential — and most misunderstood — metric in higher education. This article explains how the four major ranking systems work at a methodological level.

## QS: Reputation-First

Academic Reputation Survey (40%) drives the ranking. Over 130,000 academics rate institutions in their field. This is QS's strength (captures prestige that publications can't measure) and weakness (survey bias: older, well-known, English-language universities are over-represented; "halo effect": a university's overall prestige bleeds into subject ratings). Employer Reputation (10%) captures industry perception. Citations per Faculty (20%) measures research impact, normalised by field.

## THE: Teaching + Research Balance

THE's methodology is more balanced: Teaching (29.5% — reputation survey, staff/student ratio, doctorate/bachelor ratio, institutional income), Research (29% — reputation survey, income, productivity), Citations (30% — field-weighted citation impact), International Outlook (7.5%), Industry Income (4%). THE's strength is capturing both teaching and research. Weakness: the teaching metrics rely heavily on the reputation survey, which has the same bias problems as QS.

## ARWU: Pure Research Output

ARWU is the "publish or perish" ranking: 40% weight on Nobel Prizes/Fields Medals (alumni + staff), Highly Cited Researchers (20%), Nature & Science papers (20%), papers indexed in Web of Science (20%). ARWU is the most "objective" — no surveys, all bibliometric — but also the least relevant to undergraduate teaching quality.

## What None of These Measure

Student satisfaction, teaching quality at the program level, graduate employment outcomes by discipline, value for money, or post-study visa pathways. Rankings are an incomplete picture — essential context for their interpretation.
